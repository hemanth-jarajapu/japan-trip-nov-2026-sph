📷 Day 2 Photography Tips — Gion & Yasaka
  • Yasaka Shrine lanterns — shoot at dusk (~17:00 in November). The orange lanterns against a darkening sky are iconic. No tripod needed with modern phone cameras.
  • Gion cobblestone lanes — Hanamikoji Street is the classic. Walk south from Shijo-dori for the traditional machiya (townhouse) facades. Best shots: lamp-lit alleys after 18:00.
  • Pontocho alley — narrow lantern-lit lane. Use portrait mode / night mode. Shoot from one end for a vanishing-point perspective.
  • ⚠️ Gion rule: Photography of maiko/geiko is now restricted in private alleys. A ¥10,000 fine applies for violators. Do not follow or photograph them.
⚠️ Day 2 Precautions
  • Jet lag: You've just arrived after a long flight — pace yourself. Don't overdo Day 2.
  • SIM/eSIM: Activate at the airport before leaving — you'll need Google Maps immediately.
  • IC Card: Load at least ¥3,000–5,000 onto your Suica/PASMO immediately at Haneda.
  • Luggage at Kyoto hotel: If early check-in isn't available, most hotels hold luggage for free — ask at reception.
  • Shinkansen: Do NOT board an unreserved car with large luggage without the new luggage reservation (¥1,000 surcharge if caught without reserved space since 2024).
  • Gion private alleys: Some alleys now have ¥10,000 fines for photography. Respect the signs.

📷 Day 3 Photography Tips — Fushimi Inari, Tofuku-ji & Kiyomizu
  • Fushimi Inari torii tunnels (before 07:00): Walk ~15–20 min past the first tunnel for quieter sections. Shoot from inside looking back toward the entrance for the classic vanishing-point tunnel shot. Wait 10–15 seconds for crowds to thin. Lower angles make gates look taller.
  • Fushimi Inari — Yotsutsuji Intersection: ~30 min up from the base. Panoramic view over southern Kyoto. Best at dawn with mist. This is your turnaround point if time is short.
  • Tofuku-ji — Tsutenkyo Bridge: The classic autumn shot. Frame the bridge with maple canopy on both sides. Shoot from the Kaisando bridge to include the maple garden below. Peak foliage in November = extraordinary.
  • Kiyomizu-dera — wooden stage: The iconic shot is from the pagoda viewpoint to the left of the main hall (looking back at the stage with city and red maples behind it). Best in afternoon light ~14:00–16:00.
  • Sannenzaka / Ninenzaka: Shoot from street level early. The old stone-paved lanes with machiya on both sides are best without crowds — before 09:00 for best results.
  • November sunset: ~16:30–16:45 in Kyoto. Plan accordingly to be at your best photo spot by 15:30 for golden hour light.
  • Nanzen-ji — Suirokaku Aqueduct: Walk on top of the aqueduct (yes, it's allowed) and shoot along its length with maple trees framing both sides — a uniquely un-Japanese image in a very Japanese setting. Best angle: stand at the end looking inward with foliage framing the arch. Late afternoon light (16:00–17:00) hits the red brick beautifully.
  • Philosopher's Path: The canal runs parallel to the path — crouch low and shoot the canal reflections of maple overhangs. The most photogenic stretch is between Nanzen-ji and Eikando (southern portion). Soft, diffused late-afternoon light works best here — no harsh shadows in the narrow canal corridor.
⚠️ Day 3 Precautions
  • Fushimi Inari: Wear comfortable walking shoes — the upper trails are uneven stone steps. Early morning can be cold (~8–10°C in November) — bring a jacket.
  • Tofuku-ji: Gets extremely crowded in November autumn season (peak foliage). Go early. Entry ¥600. Check hours: typically 09:00–16:30. tofukuji.jp ↗
  • Kiyomizu-dera: Uphill walk from bus stop. Entry ¥500. Heels / slippery footwear not recommended. No photography inside the inner sanctum. Hours: 06:00–18:00 (check seasonal hours). kiyomizudera.or.jp ↗
  • Nanzen-ji: The main grounds and Suirokaku Aqueduct viewing area are free to enter. The Hojo (inner garden) is ¥500. You can walk on top of the aqueduct — it's a unique photo opportunity. Hours: 08:40–17:00 (Dec–Feb until 16:30). nanzenji.or.jp ↗
  • Philosopher's Path: A public, free walking path. The southern stretch (Nanzen-ji to Eikando) is the most scenic with maple canopy. Can be busy on weekends in November — walk it later in the day (after 17:00) for fewer crowds and beautiful ambient light.
  • Gion private alleys: Photography fines are now enforced — do not enter private alleys or photograph geisha. Hanamikoji Street's public sections are fine.
  • Eating while walking: Officially discouraged in Kyoto (part of Japan Tourism Agency guidelines). Find a bench or stall seating area.
  • Phone battery: Day 3 is the most photography-intensive day — fully charge, carry power bank.

🧳 Airports, Trains & Hotel Check-In

The "how" for the four checkpoints you'll hit repeatedly — Haneda, Shinkansen boarding, hotel check-in, and moving your luggage between cities. Day-specific timings stay on their Day panels; this is the reference for what actually happens at each step.

🛬 Haneda Arrival (Day 2)

  • Order: Immigration → Baggage claim → Customs → SIM/cash/IC card → Trains
  • Visit Japan Web (VJW) is optional, not mandatory — paper arrival/customs cards are still handed out on the flight and accepted at every counter
  • If registering VJW: do it 1–2 weeks ahead, complete both immigration and customs sections, and screenshot the QR code in case you have no signal on landing
  • Haneda uses a "Joint Kiosk" that scans your passport and QR together, clearing immigration and customs in one stop instead of two separate queues

🛫 Haneda Departure (Day 10)

  • Reach the terminal at least 3 hours before an international flight (matches Day 1/Day 10 timing)
  • Keep passport, boarding pass, and any duty-free receipts together and accessible — don't bury them in checked luggage
  • Duty-free / tax-free rules do change from time to time — confirm the current process at the store counter rather than assuming last year's rules apply
🚄 Shinkansen Boarding — Step by Step
  • Reach the platform with time to spare, find your car number marked on the platform floor, and line up there — doors open briefly, so don't wander
  • Confirm which end of the platform your car number is at using the diagrams posted near the gates or on your ticket
  • Your ticket shows car + seat number; unreserved-car tickets just need any open seat in the designated free-seating cars
⚠️ Oversized luggage rule (Tokaido Shinkansen — your Day 2 & Day 5 trains): any bag with total dimensions (height + width + depth, including wheels and handles) of 160cm or more needs a free "seat with oversized baggage area" reserved in advance. Board without reserving one and you can be charged a ¥1,000 on-the-spot fee and have your bag relocated by the conductor. Measure your suitcases at home before you fly — a bag listed as 158cm often crosses 160cm once wheels are included.

🎌 Japanese Etiquette — Essential Rules for Every Tourist
⚠️
Japan won't embarrass you for etiquette mistakes — locals are patient and respectful. But following these basics will genuinely enrich your experience and show respect for the places you're visiting.

⛩ Shrine & Temple Etiquette

  • Bow slightly when passing through the torii gate at shrines
  • Use the temizuya (water pavilion) to purify hands: ladle water, rinse left then right hand, then cup water to rinse your mouth lightly. Do not drink directly from ladle or spit into the basin.
  • At the worship hall: throw a coin into the offering box, bow twice, clap twice, then bow once more (at Shinto shrines)
  • Walk to the side of the central approach (sando) — the middle is for the deity
  • Do not touch or climb on religious statues
  • No flash photography inside sanctuaries unless clearly permitted
  • Speak quietly. Silence phones before entering.
  • Do not deface or mark any religious structure (criminal offense — arrest is possible)

🚇 Train Etiquette

  • No phone calls on local trains and subways. Put phone on silent.
  • Eat only on Shinkansen (bullet trains) — not on local trains or subways
  • Keep bags on your lap or overhead rack — not on the seat next to you
  • Don't talk loudly. Match the quiet atmosphere of those around you.
  • Let passengers exit first before boarding
  • Priority seating (silver/blue marked seats) — offer these to elderly, pregnant, or disabled passengers
  • Escalators: Stand on the LEFT in Osaka/Kyoto, RIGHT in Tokyo (Kanto standard)
  • Queue in the marked lines on train platforms

🍽 Restaurant Etiquette

  • Say "Itadakimasu" before eating
  • Say "Gochisousama deshita" when finished (to the staff/kitchen)
  • Never tip — it is considered rude in Japan
  • Use the hot towel (oshibori) to clean your hands before eating — don't use it to wipe your face
  • Don't stick chopsticks upright in rice (funeral gesture)
  • Don't pass food chopstick-to-chopstick (funeral gesture)
  • Slurping noodles and soup is completely acceptable — it's a compliment
  • Many restaurants do not allow modification of standard dishes

🗑 Trash & Cleanliness

  • Japan has very few public trash cans — carry a small bag for your litter
  • You can use the trash bins at convenience stores if you bought something there
  • Never litter — fines up to ¥50,000 exist and locals find it deeply disrespectful
  • Don't eat while walking (official government guidelines since 2024) — eat at stalls or benches
  • Sort trash: combustible, non-combustible, and recyclables. Hotels have this system.
  • Japan is remarkably clean — help keep it that way

📸 Photography Etiquette

  • Ask permission before photographing individuals — especially geisha/maiko
  • In Gion private alleys (Hanamikoji south section), photography is banned. ¥10,000 fine.
  • Never block streets, doorways, or temple paths for photos
  • No selfie sticks in most museums and indoor venues
  • Drone use is prohibited over temples, shrines, and near Mount Fuji trail areas
  • Don't stop in the middle of the Shibuya Crossing to photograph — keep moving with the crowd
  • At major attractions like Warner Bros. and art museums, check if specific rooms have photography restrictions

👟 Shoes & Indoor Spaces

  • Remove shoes when entering a private home, tatami room, or traditional restaurant with raised floor
  • Look for a step (tataki) or a shoe rack near the entrance as your cue
  • Wear clean, easy-to-remove socks — many temples and traditional spaces require shoe removal
  • Traditional ryokan and some temple interiors: shoes off at the entrance
  • Slippers provided in most places — but never wear slippers into a tatami room

🗣 General Public Behaviour

  • Keep voice levels calm in all public spaces — crowded or not
  • Avoid large public displays of affection
  • Walk on the left side of pavements (mirroring driving rules)
  • Do not cross roads on red lights even if the road is empty
  • Avoid pointing directly at people — use an open hand to indicate direction
  • A slight bow as acknowledgment goes a long way — local staff will appreciate it
  • Smoking: Only in designated smoking areas. Street smoking is illegal in most areas with fines of ¥1,000–5,000.

🏧 Cash & Payments

  • Japan is still largely a cash society — always carry some yen
  • Most convenience stores, station shops, and major restaurants accept cards
  • Smaller temples, local stalls, taxis, and older restaurants may be cash only
  • Hand money with both hands or place it in the tray — don't put money directly into someone's hand in formal settings
  • Count your change privately — don't count at the counter (implies distrust)
  • Tax-free shopping: available at many stores for purchases over ¥5,000 — show your passport

✍️ Japanese Writing Systems — Quick Primer

📖
You do not need to learn all three scripts for travel. Focus on recognising key kanji (listed in Signs tab) and understanding that katakana is used for foreign words — so foreign brand names and loanwords will feel readable.
ExampleUsed forCharactersScript
こんにちは (Konnichiwa)Japanese grammar, particles, native words46 phoneticHiragana

東京 (Tōkyō = Tokyo)Nouns, verbs, signs, place names2000+ logographsKanji
Useful tip: Foreign words written in katakana often sound like their English equivalents — e.g. バス = basu = bus, ホテル = hoteru = hotel, コンビニ = konbini = convenience store. Sound them out syllable by syllable.
